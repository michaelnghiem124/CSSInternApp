2 · Motivation

A workforce needs gap, a volunteering program for busy individuals, and enriched networking opportunities enhanced by AI

Mission

The Cyber Security Summit is an annual event hosted in Minneapolis that brings together thought leaders across the public, private, military, and not-for-profit sectors to encourage dialogue and network building among current and aspiring IT security professionals. It is organized by a team of about 50 volunteer Think Tank members who develop content programming, engage sponsors, and plan year-long events to keep the community active and engaged.

The Summit selects 12–15 interns per year to support its mission of developing the next generation of cybersecurity talent. I know the value firsthand: my own career began as a Summit intern in 2011.

The program's three outcomes

  1. A mentorship pipeline. College students and people pivoting into cybersecurity connect with leaders in this space, and those connections lead to full-time positions.
  2. Engagement with the mission. Interns take part in Think Tank Talks, year-round events, and day-of-Summit activities.
  3. Talent for Think Tank organizations. Think Tank leaders meet the next generation of cybersecurity talent so they can better staff their organizations.
2 · Motivation (continued)

The opportunity: busy volunteers, busy interns, and a manual process

How it works today

  • Scattered information. Interns receive information through a series of Slack messages, emails, and meetings, which can be hard to balance while working and in school.
  • No central view. Calendar events, intern profiles and career objectives, and who is engaged aren't in one place, which adds 2–4 hours a week of manual overhead to prepare (own estimate). This is a volunteer commitment for senior leaders, and they are busy.
  • Manual matching. Placing interns means working out each one's fit, checking which committees still have needs, and then updating a spreadsheet. With some automation and smart recommendations, there is a better chance of matching each intern with the right committee so everyone gets what they want.
  • Unpaid time. Interns choose this program on top of school and work, and it isn't compensated. As a volunteer organization, we have an obligation to maximize their time by providing the best resources for them to succeed.

Why care

21% vs. 3%
Projected U.S. job growth for information security analysts from 2025 to 2035, roughly seven times the average across all occupations (BLS). There's real opportunity there.
  • A skills gap, not just a headcount gap. Cybersecurity used to be a lucrative field for new entrants, but it's getting harder to break into: demand is shifting toward specialists, and skills gaps, not headcount, are now the profession's largest concern (ISC2, 2025). Mentorship and apprenticeship won't fill the on-demand skills gap overnight, but they foster the next generation of cyber talent.
  • Digital trust. Cybersecurity builds trust in an increasingly connected world that's changing fast as AI permeates our lives. Deepfakes, rising fraud, and automation without a human in the loop are problems we can help address by cultivating the next generation of cyber leaders.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook: Information Security Analysts (retrieved Nov 7, 2026) · ISC2, 2025 Cybersecurity Workforce Study (Dec 4, 2025).

4 · The agents · why each one exists

Two agents for interns, two workflows for the Program Lead

Mentor Match (intern)

The program's main goal is to connect interns with security mentors and coaches who might one day hire them. Before this, the intern had to do most of the work and research. Now an intern uploads a resume and gets 3–5 named Think Tank members, each with the reason, the shared ground and conversation starters, plus upcoming talks and openings at those members' organizations.

Committee placements (Program Lead)

After selecting interns, the first job is deciding which committee each one supports. The agents propose a ranked matrix that already respects capacity and meeting times. A human makes the final call. Then the Intro Drafter writes the welcome email and Slack DM.

Cohort Pulse (Program Lead)

Shows how engaged each intern is: which meetings they attend and how active they are in Slack. The brief flags who needs a nudge before they drift. Names are swapped for pseudonyms before the AI sees anything.

Opportunities (both)

Interns can filter, or just ask, “who's hiring?” Each opening names the Think Tank member at that employer, so the intern can write a warm, skills-matched note instead of a cold application.

Which models, and why

List prices, Nov 2026, as set in backend/config.py.

  • Sonnet where judgment matters. Matching means weighing 64 bios against one person, using tools and following hard rules. A wrong pick costs the intern a real relationship.
  • Haiku where the job is extraction or labeling. It's 20× cheaper per token, and reading a resume into a fixed schema doesn't need deep reasoning.
  • Raw resume text never reaches Sonnet. Haiku turns it into a compact profile first.
  • Thinking effort is set to low on every agent. Structured output plus code validators catch mistakes more cheaply than extra reasoning would.

  7. Follow-ups. 4 more turns, including a resume re-upload. Whole session: 7 model runs, $0.10.

5 · Tokenomics · controls

How cost is kept down, and tracked

Cost levers in the code

  • Model tiering: Haiku reads and labels; Sonnet only matches.
  • Prompt caching of the instructions + 64-member roster: 79% of matcher input comes from the cache.
  • Low thinking effort and max_tokens = 4,000.
  • Pay once: fit profiles and job classifications are cached by content hash.
  • Code before AI: regex title filter, signals computed in code for Pulse, and a free plain-code “Best fit for me” sort.
  • Hard caps: 8 model calls / 10 tool calls / 120k tokens per run; 200 classifications per refresh.

Admin dashboard

Admin → AI usage tracks every query: agent, model, input / cached / output tokens, cost, latency and outcome. It also shows overall usage against the daily budget and blocked safety events. Each Mentor Match reply also shows the intern its own token count and cost.

6 · Safety

What could go wrong, and what stops it

RiskDefense built into the app
Strangers using the appEverything is behind sign-in. Interns can register only with a program access code from the Program Lead (rotatable). Passwords are 12+ characters and hashed with scrypt. Sign-in and registration are rate-limited. The admin account has no default password.
Account takeover via password reset“Forgot password” emails a one-time 16-character temporary password that expires in 24 h. Every page stays blocked until a new 12+ character password is set. The old password keeps working until then, so nobody can lock an intern out. Every other device gets signed out.
Prompt injection (chat, resumes, Slack, job posts)Layer 1: a regex screen removes instruction-like lines before any model sees them. Layer 2: the Profile Reader flags hidden instructions. Layer 3: prompts treat uploaded content as data. Layer 4: output validators.
Agent invents factsThe Mentor Matcher can only name people and events that exist, because code rejects anything else and forces a retry. Placement output must respect capacity and schedules.
Agent takes the wrong actionNo agent can send anything. Intro emails and Slack DMs are drafts. Pulse tools are read-only. A human approves every placement.
Leaking intern dataPulse sees only pseudonyms (Intern-03). A validator rejects briefs containing names, emails or phones. Phone numbers are visible only to the admin. Photo EXIF data, including GPS, is stripped.
Loops and overspendingPer run: 8 model calls, 10 tool calls, 120k tokens. Per session: 6 messages a minute, 30 turns, 4,000-character messages, 15k-character resumes, 2 MB uploads. App-wide: a 2M-token daily budget, after which AI features pause until midnight and the rest of the app keeps working.

8 · Limits + next steps

What doesn't work yet, and the next month

Limits today

  • Not tested with real interns yet. All runs so far used sample and demo data.
  • No quality eval yet, so match quality is judged by eye.
  • The injection screen is a pattern list. Reworded attacks can get past layer 1, and the later layers have to catch them.
  • Single server: the rate limiter lives in memory and resets on restart; storage is SQLite; there is one admin account.
  • Chats aren't saved: they last 3 hours in server memory.
  • Live Slack sync needs a bot token. Federal (USAJOBS) postings need an API key.
  • The 2M-token daily budget is sized for one cohort, not thousands of users.

With another month

  • Pilot with the previous cohort: collect user-experience feedback and use it to decide which features to build next.
  • Recorded sessions on the hub: post Think Tank Talk recordings automatically so interns who missed a talk can watch.
  • Transcripts: record talks, export a transcript and summary, and feed key takeaways into Mentor Match.
  • Quality eval: a fixed test set of resumes, rerun on every prompt or model change.
  • Deploy with per-user quotas and persistent storage.
